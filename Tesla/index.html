01

Early Life & Formation

Nikola Tesla was born on July 10, 1856, in Smiljan, a village in the Military Frontier of the Austrian Empire (modern-day Croatia). His father, Milutin Tesla, was a Serbian Orthodox priest; his mother, Georgina (Djuka), though unschooled, was an inventive woman who built household tools and memorized Serbian epic poetry.

Tesla showed extraordinary mental abilities from childhood—he could perform integral calculus in his head and possessed an eidetic memory so vivid he could visualize complete machines, rotate them, and test them mentally before building a single part.

After studying at the Austrian Polytechnic in Graz (where he first saw a Gramme dynamo and conceived the idea for AC motors) and the University of Prague, Tesla worked briefly for the Budapest Telephone Exchange before joining Edison's operations in Paris in 1882.

Serbian Heritage

Born into a family of Orthodox clergy and military officers on the Croatian frontier. His older brother Dane's death in childhood deeply affected the young Nikola.

The Graz Revelation

Watching a DC Gramme dynamo spark at its commutator, Tesla told his professor it could work without brushes. The professor called it impossible. Tesla would prove him wrong.

Budapest Epiphany (1882)

While walking in a park, reciting Goethe's Faust, the complete design of the rotating magnetic field and AC induction motor appeared in Tesla's mind—fully formed.

02

Career & Key Moments

Arrival in America (1884)

Tesla arrived in New York with four cents, a letter of introduction to Edison, and a head full of AC designs. He worked for Edison briefly, but their visions clashed fundamentally—Edison was committed to DC, Tesla to AC.

The Westinghouse Alliance (1888)

George Westinghouse purchased Tesla's AC patents for $60,000 plus royalties. This partnership powered the War of Currents against Edison and would electrify the world with alternating current.

Colorado Springs (1899)

Tesla built a massive experimental station to study high-voltage, high-frequency phenomena. He generated artificial lightning, claimed to receive extraterrestrial signals, and advanced his wireless power transmission theories.

Wardenclyffe & Decline (1901–1943)

Tesla's ambitious Wardenclyffe Tower project, intended for transatlantic wireless communication and power transmission, lost funding when J.P. Morgan withdrew support. Tesla spent his final decades in increasing isolation, though never ceasing to invent.

03

Historical Context

The War of Currents

The 1880s and 1890s saw one of the most consequential technological battles in history: DC vs. AC. Thomas Edison had built an empire on direct current, constructing power stations that served small districts of Manhattan. But DC could not be transmitted efficiently over long distances.

Tesla and Westinghouse championed alternating current, which could be stepped up to high voltage via transformers for efficient long-distance transmission, then stepped back down for safe use. Edison fought back with a propaganda campaign, publicly electrocuting animals with AC to demonstrate its "danger."

The decisive victory came in 1893 when Westinghouse, using Tesla's polyphase system, won the contract to light the Chicago World's Fair—and in 1896, when AC power from Niagara Falls reached Buffalo, 26 miles away.

Edison's Campaign

Edison promoted the electric chair (using AC) and staged public electrocutions of animals. Harold Brown, Edison's associate, coined "Westinghoused" as a synonym for electrocution.

The 1893 World's Fair

Tesla's polyphase system illuminated the entire fair with 200,000 light bulbs. The spectacle proved AC's superiority to millions of visitors and sealed the outcome of the current war.

Niagara Falls (1896)

Tesla's AC generators harnessed the falls' power and transmitted it to Buffalo—the first large-scale AC power system, proving that electricity could be delivered across distances economically.

04

Alternating Current & Polyphase Motor

The AC Revolution

Tesla's fundamental insight was that a rotating magnetic field could be created by multiple phases of alternating current, offset in time. This rotating field would drag a rotor along with it—no brushes, no commutator, no sparking.

His polyphase AC system included generators, transformers, transmission lines, and motors—a complete ecosystem for electrical power. The key innovation: using two or three phases of AC, each offset by 90 or 120 degrees, to create smooth, continuous rotation.

This was not merely an improvement over DC—it was a paradigm shift that made long-distance power transmission practical and enabled the electrification of the world.

05

The Polyphase System in Depth

Tesla's AC patents (filed 1887–1888) described not just a motor but an entire power system—generation, transformation, transmission, and utilization.

The Induction Motor

Tesla's polyphase induction motor uses a rotating magnetic field produced by stator windings carrying phase-shifted AC currents. The rotor, typically a squirrel cage, follows the field through electromagnetic induction—no electrical connection to the rotor is needed. Simple, robust, and efficient.

Transformer Principle

AC can be stepped up to thousands of volts for transmission (reducing I²R losses) and stepped back down for end use. This is impossible with DC. Tesla's system exploited this fully, making power delivery over hundreds of miles economically viable.

40 Patents in 1888

Tesla filed over 40 patents covering polyphase AC systems. His May 1888 lecture at the American Institute of Electrical Engineers presented the rotating magnetic field concept and demonstrated working motors, electrifying the engineering world.

Westinghouse at Niagara

The 1896 Niagara Falls installation used Tesla's polyphase generators to produce 15,000 horsepower of electrical energy. Power reached Buffalo 26 miles away—a triumph of AC that ended the current war decisively.

"I have not only convinced myself of this but have also proved that alternating current is the only form of current by which electrification of the whole globe can be accomplished."

— Nikola Tesla, lecture to the AIEE, 1888
06

The Tesla Coil & High-Frequency Resonance

Resonant Transformers

In 1891, Tesla invented the Tesla coil—a resonant transformer circuit that produces extremely high voltages at high frequencies. The device operates on a principle Tesla understood deeply: electrical resonance.

A primary circuit (capacitor + spark gap + few-turn coil) drives a loosely coupled secondary coil with many turns. When the primary and secondary resonate at the same frequency, energy transfers efficiently, producing voltages of hundreds of thousands to millions of volts.

Tesla used these coils to study high-frequency phenomena, wireless energy transmission, fluorescent lighting, and X-rays—often years before others received credit for similar discoveries.

07

High-Frequency Mastery

The Tesla coil was not a parlor trick—it was a research instrument that opened the door to radio, wireless power, and modern high-frequency electronics.

Resonance Principle

Tesla deeply understood that electrical circuits, like mechanical systems, have natural frequencies. By matching the resonant frequency of his primary and secondary circuits (f = 1/2π√LC), he achieved enormous voltage amplification with minimal input power.

Colorado Springs (1899)

Tesla built a 60-foot Tesla coil that produced 12 million volts and artificial lightning bolts over 130 feet long. He used the facility to study Earth's electrical resonance and wireless transmission over distances.

Fluorescent Lighting

Tesla demonstrated wireless fluorescent lamps powered by high-frequency fields years before commercial fluorescent lighting existed. He showed that high-frequency currents could safely pass through the human body.

X-Ray Observations

Tesla produced X-ray images as early as 1894, before Roentgen's 1895 announcement. A laboratory fire destroyed much of his evidence, and he graciously acknowledged Roentgen's independent discovery.

08

Radio Transmission & Wireless Power

Tesla was among the earliest pioneers of radio communication, demonstrating wireless transmission years before Marconi's famous transatlantic signal.

Radio Patents (1897–1900)

Tesla filed fundamental radio patents in 1897 describing tuned circuits for wireless telegraphy. His 1898 demonstration of a radio-controlled boat at Madison Square Garden stunned audiences—it was the world's first remote-controlled device.

Tesla vs. Marconi

Marconi's 1901 transatlantic transmission used technology covered by Tesla's patents. In 1943, the US Supreme Court posthumously restored Tesla's radio patent priority over Marconi, recognizing Tesla's foundational contributions.

Wardenclyffe Tower (1901)

Tesla's most ambitious project: a 187-foot tower on Long Island designed for transatlantic wireless communication and, ultimately, wireless power distribution. J.P. Morgan funded construction, but withdrew support when Tesla revealed his power transmission ambitions.

Wireless Power Vision

Tesla envisioned a global wireless power system using the Earth and ionosphere as conductors. While his specific scheme was physically impractical at scale, the concept of wireless power transfer has been realized in modern technologies like inductive charging.

The Eidetic Engineer

Tesla described his mental process in detail: he would conceive an idea, construct it entirely in his imagination, mentally operate it for weeks, then check for signs of wear. When he finally built the physical device, it worked as imagined—often on the first attempt.

Showmanship as Strategy

Tesla was a master of public demonstration. His 1891 lecture at Columbia University, where he held glowing tubes of gas lit wirelessly by high-frequency fields, made him the most famous electrical inventor in America and attracted essential funding.

Rivalry & Controversy

Edison, Marconi, and the Myth

Tesla's rivalry with Edison has become legendary—and somewhat mythologized. Edison genuinely opposed AC and fought its adoption through dubious means. But the personal animosity is often overstated; Tesla later expressed respect for Edison's work ethic, if not his methods.

The Marconi controversy is more clear-cut. Marconi's Nobel Prize for radio (1909) relied on Tesla's patented tuned circuits. The US Supreme Court's 1943 decision in Tesla's favor came too late for Tesla himself, who had died months earlier.

In later life, Tesla made increasingly grandiose claims—death rays, communication with Mars, unlimited free energy—which damaged his scientific credibility. Modern admirers sometimes struggle to separate Tesla's genuine brilliance from his more speculative assertions.

"The present is theirs; the future, for which I really worked, is mine."

— Nikola Tesla

The $50,000 Question

Tesla claimed Edison promised him $50,000 to improve his DC generators. Upon completion, Edison allegedly said, "Tesla, you don't understand our American humor." Whether strictly true, the anecdote captures their fundamental incompatibility.

The Royalty Sacrifice

When Westinghouse faced financial difficulty, Tesla reportedly tore up his AC royalty contract—worth potentially millions. Whether entirely voluntary or negotiated, this decision left Tesla without the fortune his inventions deserved.

12

Legacy in Modern Physics & Engineering

The Tesla (T) — SI Unit

The SI unit of magnetic flux density was named the tesla in 1960. MRI machines operate at 1.5–3 T; the Earth's magnetic field is about 50 μT. Every measurement of magnetic field strength honors his name.

Global AC Power Grid

Every power outlet in the world delivers alternating current using Tesla's polyphase system. The entire global electrical infrastructure—generation, transmission, distribution—operates on principles he patented in the 1880s.

Electric Motors Everywhere

Tesla's induction motor design drives industrial machinery, electric vehicles, washing machines, HVAC systems, and countless other applications. It remains the most widely used electric motor type, prized for its simplicity and reliability.

Wireless Power Renaissance

Tesla's dream of wireless power is being realized through resonant inductive coupling in smartphone chargers, electric vehicle pads, and medical implants—using the exact resonance principles he demonstrated over a century ago.

13

Applications Today

Electric Vehicles

Modern EVs use AC induction motors directly descended from Tesla's 1888 design. Tesla, Inc. chose its name in tribute. The motor's efficiency and simplicity make it ideal for automotive applications.

Wireless Charging

Qi chargers for phones and inductive pads for EVs use resonant coupling—the same principle Tesla demonstrated when he lit wireless lamps across a room in the 1890s.

Power Transmission

High-voltage AC transmission lines spanning continents carry power from remote generating stations to cities. Every transformer on every utility pole uses the AC voltage conversion Tesla championed.

Radio & Communications

Tesla's tuned circuit patents underlie all radio technology. Modern cell towers, Wi-Fi routers, and Bluetooth devices use frequency-selective resonant circuits derived from his fundamental work.

Robotics & Remote Control

Tesla's 1898 radio-controlled boat was the first teleautomaton. Modern drones, remote surgery robots, and autonomous vehicles trace their conceptual lineage to his Madison Square Garden demonstration.

MRI & Medical Tech

MRI scanners measure magnetic flux in tesla units, and their RF coils operate on resonance principles Tesla pioneered. Implantable medical devices increasingly use wireless power transfer he envisioned.

Recommended Reading

My Inventions: The Autobiography of Nikola Tesla

Nikola Tesla (1919, serialized). Tesla's own account of his life and creative process, originally published in Electrical Experimenter. Vivid, eccentric, and essential primary source material.

Tesla: Inventor of the Electrical Age

W. Bernard Carlson (2013). The definitive modern biography, meticulously researched and balanced. Carlson examines Tesla's inventions in technical detail while placing them in social and economic context.

Wizard: The Life and Times of Nikola Tesla

Marc Seifer (1996). A comprehensive biography drawing on previously unavailable sources, covering Tesla's entire life from Smiljan to the Hotel New Yorker.

Empires of Light

Jill Jonnes (2003). The story of the War of Currents told through Edison, Tesla, and Westinghouse. Vivid narrative history of how AC triumphed and electrified America.

The Nikola Tesla Patent Collection

Various editors. Collections of Tesla's 300+ patents reveal the depth and breadth of his inventive output—from AC power systems to radio to turbines to logic gates.

Tesla: Man Out of Time

Margaret Cheney (1981). An accessible popular biography that helped revive public interest in Tesla during the 1980s. Good introduction for general readers.
